3. Findings

  • No learner replicates the exact solver. On the random board the exact solver wins 67% from the ball seat, 39% from the other seat and 53% balanced; the six learners win 5% to 14%, 0% to 1% and 2% to 8%. On the deterministic board the exact solver ties itself and the learners win 2% or less; in the continuing game every learner wins 0%.
  • Against a random player the learners win 61% to 96% of games (deterministic board); fictitious play does better than standard training.
  • Starting from the 94 states where the exact answer has to mix, the learners win 11% to 15% against the exact Nash policy; the exact solver wins 50%.
  • Best settings, on 10 seeds: A2C fictitious play at 10,000 iterations wins 30% from the ball seat (95% interval 24% to 36%) against 9% at 2,000; PPO with replayed data wins 28% (13% to 44%) against 10%. Both gains hold up and stay well below the exact solver.

Standard training keeps swinging; the fictitious-play average is steadier but flat and low.

5. Rock-paper-scissors with neural networks

Each player is a small softmax network trained by REINFORCE (3,000 iterations, 64 games each, 3 seeds; seed 0 shown). Orange: the current network's share of rock. Green: the average over past snapshots (fictitious play), of softmax policies or of argmax (pure) policies. Exploitability (right) is 0 at one third each. With the soccer settings the standard networks cycle (1.48) and neither average reaches one third (softmax 1.19, argmax 1.16). With a much larger entropy bonus (0.2) and plain gradient steps all three settle: exploitability 0.10 standard, 0.07 softmax average, 0.28 argmax average. That is a stabilization effect of the changed objective, not evidence of convergence to the unregularized equilibrium.

6. Training choices

Each comparison changes one thing from the usual setting (2,000 iterations of 64 episodes, a snapshot every 5 iterations, all 100 steps in the loss) on 3 seeds. Changes are in percentage points; single seeds of one setting differ by 10 to 30 points, so differences of that size are noise.

  • One shared network (two output heads), random board: −3 to +11 points.
  • Last 10 of 100 steps left out, random board: −8 to +4 points; PPO with standard training stops scoring.
  • Averaging argmax (pure) snapshots instead of softmax ones: −6 to −4 points on the random board; no gain on the other boards.
  • Entropy bonus 0.2 instead of 0.01 (an ablation; it changes the optimization problem, so it does not show convergence to the unregularized equilibrium), random board: REINFORCE −4, A2C −6, PPO +24 points.
  • Leaving out the last steps: how many?: win rate, change in points from "all steps": last 5 left out −10 to +11; last 10 left out −8 to +4; last 20 left out −9 to +11; last 40 left out −10 to +13.
  • Leaving out the last steps in the continuing game: share tied with the exact Nash policy, change in points from "all steps": last 5 left out −59 to −43; last 10 left out −67 to −15; last 20 left out −66 to −44; last 40 left out −40 to −33.
  • How long to train between snapshots: win rate, change in points from "snapshot every 5 iterations (used elsewhere)": every iteration −6 to −2; every 25 −11 to −3; every 100 −10 to +4.
  • Five times as many iterations: win rate, change in points from "2,000 iterations": 10,000 iterations −9 to +23.
  • Mini-batches and replayed data: win rate, change in points from "all data, one step": mini-batches of 256 −11 to +6; replay of 4 iterations −2 to +37.
  • The exact solver's values as the critic: random board: win rate, change in points from "learned critic": exact critic −8 to −5.
  • The exact solver's values as the critic: deterministic board: share tied with the exact Nash policy, change in points from "learned critic": exact critic −43 to +0.
  • Averaging weights instead of policies: win rate, change in points from "average of policies": average of weights −7 to −3; average of the output layer's weights −4 to +1.

7. Related work

Has fictitious play been combined with policy gradient before? Yes, in several forms, and the convergence theory is much older than the neural networks. I checked the titles, authors and venues against Google Scholar (its top result for fictitious play, policy gradient and zero-sum Markov games was Liu, Yang and Wang) and compared each one-line summary with the abstract on the paper's own proceedings, journal or arXiv page, or on a bibliographic mirror where the publisher's page would not load (noted in the entry). JSTOR and the publisher pages for the 1951 papers would not load, so Brown and Robinson are cited from the linked secondary source and were not read. I have not checked the proofs. I did not find a result that proves convergence for deep policy gradient with fictitious play in zero-sum Markov games; the closest results are tabular, regularized or for special game classes.

Older work

  • Iterative solution of games by fictitious play (Brown, 1951; Activity Analysis of Production and Allocation). Introduced fictitious play as a computer method for finding equilibria of two-player zero-sum games (first proposed in 1949). Not read directly (the publisher and JSTOR pages did not load); described in the linked paper.
  • An iterative method of solving a game (Robinson, 1951; Annals of Mathematics 54(2)). Proved Brown's conjecture that fictitious play converges in two-player zero-sum games; Shapley (1964) later showed that convergence fails in general games. Not read directly (JSTOR did not load); described in the linked paper.
  • Markov games as a framework for multi-agent reinforcement learning (Littman, 1994; ICML, pp. 157-163). Extends reinforcement learning from one adaptive agent to several; studies two agents with directly opposed goals sharing an environment and gives a Q-learning-style algorithm (minimax-Q), shown on a simple game whose best strategy is probabilistic.
  • Fictitious play property for games with identical interests (Monderer and Shapley, 1996; Journal of Economic Theory 68(1), pp. 258-265). Defines n-person games with identical interests (best-response equivalent in mixed strategies to a game with identical payoff functions) and proves that every such game has the fictitious-play property. Abstract read on a bibliographic mirror; the publisher's page was blocked.
  • Potential games (Monderer and Shapley, 1996; Games and Economic Behavior 14(1)). Defines several notions of potential function for games in strategic form and characterizes the games that have one: all players' incentives to change strategy are captured by a single function.
  • Multiagent learning using a variable learning rate (Bowling and Veloso, 2002; Artificial Intelligence 136(2)). Gradient-style learners in stochastic games: the WoLF (win or learn fast) learning-rate rule, with a convergence proof in self-play for a restricted class of matrix games.
  • On the global convergence of stochastic fictitious play (Hofbauer and Sandholm, 2002; Econometrica 70(6)). Stochastic (perturbed) fictitious play converges globally in four classes of games: those with an interior evolutionarily stable state, zero-sum games, potential games and supermodular games.
  • Nash Q-learning for general-sum stochastic games (Hu and Wellman, 2003; Journal of Machine Learning Research 4). Q-learning for general-sum Markov games in which each agent assumes Nash play on the current Q-values; proved to converge under restrictions on the intermediate stage games, which grid-game experiments suggest are not always necessary.
  • Generalised weakened fictitious play (Leslie and Collins, 2006; Games and Economic Behavior 56(2)). A broad class of adaptive learning processes extending weakened fictitious play, shown to converge in games with the fictitious-play property; includes an actor-critic process in which a player sees only the reward of the action it chose. Abstract read on the authors' Bristol page.
  • Brown's original fictitious play (Berger, 2007; Journal of Economic Theory 135(1)). Brown's 1951 process updates beliefs in a different order from the version used today; with that order, a short convergence proof for nondegenerate ordinal potential games.

Recent work

  • Fictitious Self-Play in Extensive-Form Games (Heinrich, Lanctot and Silver, 2015; ICML, PMLR 37). Fictitious play in behavioural strategies of extensive-form games, and a sample-based version (fictitious self-play); approximate Nash equilibria in poker games.
  • Deep Reinforcement Learning from Self-Play in Imperfect-Information Games (Heinrich and Silver, 2016; arXiv). Neural fictitious self-play (NFSP) combines fictitious self-play with deep reinforcement learning to approximate Nash equilibria end to end; on Leduc poker it converged near an equilibrium where standard reinforcement learning methods failed to converge, and on Limit Texas Hold'em it came close to leading algorithms that rely on expert knowledge.
  • Independent Policy Gradient Methods for Competitive Reinforcement Learning (Daskalakis, Foster and Golowich, 2020; NeurIPS 33). Both players running policy gradient simultaneously reach a min-max equilibrium of a two-player zero-sum stochastic game, provided the learning rates follow a two-timescale rule (which the authors show is necessary).
  • Policy Optimization in Zero-Sum Markov Games: Fictitious Self-Play Provably Attains Nash Equilibria (Liu, Yang and Wang, 2020; OpenReview). A smooth fictitious self-play in which each player takes an entropy-regularized proximal policy optimization (PPO) step against the other; the joint policies converge to a neighbourhood of a Nash equilibrium at a sublinear rate, the first finite-time guarantee for fictitious self-play in zero-sum Markov games (for the regularized method, not the exact equilibrium). Authors and year from the Google Scholar listing; the paper's own page did not load.
  • Provably Efficient Fictitious Play Policy Optimization for Zero-Sum Markov Games with Structured Transitions (Qiu, Wei, Ye, Wang and Yang, 2021; ICML, PMLR 139). Fictitious-play policy optimization with UCB-style optimism for two-player zero-sum Markov games with structured, unknown transitions (factored independent or single-controller), with tight regret bounds of order the square root of the number of rounds.
  • Policy Optimization for Markov Games: Unified Framework and Faster Convergence (Zhang, Liu, Wang, Xiong, Li and Bai, 2022; NeurIPS 35). Each iteration updates the policy at every state with a matrix-game algorithm and then the values; the state-wise average policy converges to an approximate Nash equilibrium of two-player zero-sum Markov games when the matrix-game learners have low weighted regret (rates of roughly T^-5/6, and T^-1 with a modified value update).
  • Fictitious play in zero-sum stochastic games (Sayin, Parise and Ozdaglar, 2022; SIAM Journal on Control and Optimization 60(4), pp. 2095-2114). Combines classical fictitious play with Q-learning in two-player zero-sum stochastic games; the strategy beliefs converge to a stationary mixed Nash equilibrium, in both the model-based and model-free cases.
  • Fictitious Play in Markov Games with Single Controller (Sayin, Zhang and Ozdaglar, 2022; ACM EC). A new variant of fictitious play for Markov games converges to a Nash equilibrium in n-player identical-interest games where a single player controls the transitions; together with earlier results it covers both zero-sum and single-controller identical-interest Markov games.
  • Fast Policy Extragradient Methods for Competitive Games with Entropy Regularization (Cen, Wei and Chi, 2024; Journal of Machine Learning Research 25(4)). Extragradient methods reach the entropy-regularized equilibrium at a linear rate (also in zero-sum Markov games), and tuning the regularization weight approaches an approximate Nash equilibrium of the unregularized game at a sublinear rate.
